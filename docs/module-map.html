02 — All 246 files, sorted

The inventory

Three rings, and the test for which one a thing belongs to is a question about failure rather than size: if this were deleted, what breaks? Everything, including an empty workspace, means kernel. One area of the product means capability. One way of reaching the product means adapter.

Kernel — 21 140 lines, 30% Capability — 32 594 lines, 46% Adapter — 15 497 lines, 22% Shell — 1 852 lines, 3%

Two rows were the whole diagnosis a second time. notifications was 58 lines — writing a notification is one small function, because everything it meant had been distributed into repo.ts and everything it did into five adapters. It is 296 lines now, and the growth is the fix: the meaning came home when repo started offering onCommitted, and the delivery stayed in the adapters but through a port they register with. And adapter/mcp is 5 768: the assistant's view of the product is larger than the largest thing the product actually does. Fifteen files rather than three, now, which makes it readable — not smaller. Finding 7 is why.

03 — Ordered by what they cost

Seven findings

Each with the measurement behind it and the fix it implies. The numbering is a ranking, not a sequence — the order they are fixed in is further down, and it is different.

The numbers in this section are records, not measurements. Every bold figure on this page is one or the other and check:figures will not let it be neither: a measurement is claimed against the tree and fails the build when the two disagree; a record is listed in HISTORY with a line saying what it is a record of. These are records — 2 370 lines, 1 030 selector runs — and correcting one to today's value would delete the evidence for the argument it belongs to.

1

lib/mcp.ts was a second application

5 464 lines → 13 fileslargest now 1 14972 tools, unchanged126 SQL statements

One TOOLS array, eleven groups, and the eleven groups map one-to-one onto capabilities that already exist. Adding a capability meant editing this file; removing one meant editing this file. It is a directory now — an envelope of 265 lines, a shared kit of 1 149, and eleven files of 190 to 740. The helpers are what made the split work rather than the tools: they were what the groups shared, so pulling them out left each group as a plain array that reads like a list of what an assistant can do.

Nothing about the surface moved: 72 tools, the same names, none missing and none doubled — checked against the list from before, and again through the browser, where the walkthrough asserts a freshly authorised token reaches 72 tools.

Both of the repository's layering violations were here too — mcp.ts importing storeFile and searchWorkspace from route files. Fixed: both moved down into lib/uploads.ts and lib/search.ts, where the route and the MCP tool are equal callers of one implementation. There are now no layering exceptions at all, which is worth more than two — nothing is left to cite as precedent.

Remaining. The eleven files sit under the adapter rather than beside the capability whose product they expose, and the module map says so. This note used to call moving tools/budgets.ts next to budgets step 10's job; step 10 came and went, put every file under <ring>/<module>/ and left these where they were, so it is nobody's until somebody takes it — and it still wants ToolDef to have a home a capability may import without importing an adapter. A switched-off feature also still loads its tools; assembling the list per workspace is a behaviour change and has no place in a refactor.

2

lib/repo.ts contained the rules it should dispatch to

2 370 → 1 282 linesall 8 domains moved194-line switch gone219 tests hold it

The merge, the transaction and the tombstones genuinely belong together. The budget invariants, the KPI cascades, the landscape detachments and the rate rules did not. writeEntity has the hook points now — repo.onEntity takes a rule naming the entities it speaks for, filling in whichever of three moments it cares about: defaults on create, invariants on every write, effects once the row is written — plus guards, which came with the core domains. All eight have moved, each file claimed by its own capability rather than by the kernel: applyInvariants is gone entirely, down from 194 lines to a single dispatch at its call site.

guards is the one that needed care. Chat's three checks and guardTransition are permission checks, and each was an if (entity === … && !opts.system) branch. The !opts.system half belongs to the hook now rather than to each rule, because a check a module has to remember to gate is a check that will be forgotten. It does not make a permission boundary optional: a rule that is not registered is not there at all, which wiring.ts and its test exist to prevent.

What made this mechanical rather than a re-derivation of every rule is one sentence: one write is one entity. Two rules for different entities never both run, so their relative order cannot matter — only the order within an entity, which is the order they are registered in, which is the order they were branches in. Every moved line is verbatim, and not registering them fails 29 tests for the switchable four and 190 for the core four.

The finding underneath. Two moved rules failed to announce themselves: a budget line follows its budget's workspace and a KPI reading follows its KPI's, and no test could tell either from doing nothing, because guardReferences refuses a cross-workspace parent one step earlier. They are not doing nothing — a system write skips that guard, and imports, restores and transfers all take it. They have a test now, itself checked by disabling each rule.

What a mechanical move damages quietly. The first extractor counted braces from the first line of a statement, which is wrong for a multi-line condition — it truncated two blocks and left dangling braces, and that attempt was reset rather than patched. The subtler one: three // comments were left standing over code that had gone. Losing the reasoning is the damage nothing fails on, so the check is to compare every comment line in the old file against the new set and require the difference to be empty.

Remaining. Sixteen branches, in notify, fireWebhooks and applyCreateDefaults — which are modules/notifications/effects.ts, adapters/webhooks/effects.ts and repo.ts since the write path handed the first two out. A different thing from a domain rule — the same three effects for every entity with per-entity field mapping — and finding 7 is the shape that untangles them.

3

The write path and the rules engine held each other up

repo → automation → bootstrap → repofixed0 knots left

The relationship is real — the write path calls the rules engine and the rules engine writes rows. Only the import was accidental, and being precise about which part mattered, because the obvious fix was wrong. afterWrite said so in its own comment: runAutomations stays inline on purpose, because what a rule writes must land inside the transaction. Publishing on the bus and subscribing would have made that asynchronous and broken create_tasks_batch's every task or none for exactly the rows a rule generated.

Fixed by dependency inversion, not asynchrony. repo exposes onWrite — an array of functions called in order, where runAutomations used to be. Same place, still synchronous, still inside the transaction. Only the import direction moved.

The cost, and what pays it. The new shape has a way to go wrong the old one did not: a binary that writes rows without wiring the listeners up runs no rules, silently. lib/wiring.ts is the one list of what hangs off the write path — and where each capability's effects will register, so step 7 needs more registrations rather than a new mechanism. wiring.test.ts checks that list against the files and the scripts in package.json. That the wiring is load-bearing was checked by removing it: eight of thirteen automation tests fail.

4

Three capabilities on the client held each other up

budget → insights → rates → budget2 knots fixed53 files in one folder

Budgets borrowed a table from planning, planning borrowed may this person see money from time, and time borrowed a money input back from budgets. A second knot, views.tsx ↔ saved-views.tsx, was a view's shape living in the screen that renders it.

Both fixed, and none of it needed the callers rearranged — only the shared thing moved down to where all of them could reach it: asMoney and MoneyInput into components/ui/money.tsx, the chart table into components/ui/table.tsx, useSeesMoney into session.tsx where it only ever read a role, and ViewConfig into task-parts.tsx, which both files already imported GroupBy from.

Closed by step 10. The folder of 53 components is kernel/design-system now — 23 files, the primitives under ui/ — and the cross-capability imports it worried about are no longer invisible: a project page hosting the KPI and budget panels is a row in the table further down, kept acyclic by rule 6.

5

Nothing was code-split, so every workspace downloaded every feature

1 304 kB → 819 kB385 kB → 253 kB gzipped−37%

For a workspace with the optional features off, the first load carried the budget screens, the KPI screens, the register, the timesheet, the manual and two locale catalogues nobody had selected — a sixth of the source tree, parsed on a phone that would never render a word of it. The switch already existed and lib/nav.ts already read it: a workspace without budgets got no link to budgets and all of the code anyway. Fixed.

Three things this needed that add React.lazy does not say, and all three were load-bearing. English stays static — it is the fallback translate reaches for and the catalogue the key type derives from, so main.tsx awaits the detected locale before the first render rather than showing a frame of English. A capability's panels count as the capability: routes/projects.tsx imported the KPI panels statically, which pulled all 1 676 lines of that screen straight back in and cancelled the split; they are fetched now, behind the switch at the call site, because a check inside the component runs after the import. And splitting would have cost offline — the first thing this project promises.

The offline part. The service worker caches /assets/ on first request, so a chunk nobody opened is not on the device and Settings on a train would have found nothing. The screens are warmed in requestIdleCallback once the app is up, narrowed by the same switches the navigation uses: off the critical path, on disk a second later. And because a chunk can still be missing after a deploy replaces the file an open tab remembers, the route switch sits in an error boundary that says so and offers a reload.

6

The client store recomputed everything on every change

103 selector runs per keystrokenow 10193 call sites

One counter for the whole cache: any write bumped it, every listener fired, and all 193 useQuery call sites recomputed. A chat message typed in another channel invalidated the budget dashboard's selectors — and worse than the scan itself, handed every caller a freshly allocated array, so each useMemo keyed on one fell over too. Measured on a board with a task sheet open, ten writes to one task row cost 1 030 selector runs.

Fixed with a counter per table — and a read set nobody declares. The counters were the easy half. The plan said useQuery(fn, deps, ['task', 'state']), and that is the shape that fails: a caller lists what it thinks it reads, someone adds a byId('label', …) three calls deep in a helper, and the screen quietly stops updating. No compiler catches it. So the read set is observed: list and byId are the only ways into the cache, each records its table into whatever selector is running, and the memo keys on the sum of those counters. The same ten writes now cost 100 runs.

What did not change. The component still re-renders on every write. 89 reads go straight from a render body into the cache, and the global re-render is the only thing telling them a row moved — narrowing the subscription first would be a staleness bug in 89 places, not an optimisation.

7

Reads had no home, so the assistant and the screen disagreed

359 distinct SELECTs21 written twice1 real definition

The cost was not duplicated code but duplicated definitions. The MCP tool deadlines_at_risk weighed overdue, blocked, unstarted and unassigned into a severity; the chip on a task row painted a due date red if due < today and looked at nothing else. The screen's answer was also wrong, and had been all along — a task shipped in January stayed red for ever. Nobody had noticed, because the class it set had no effect either: .due-overdue was a hand-written rule, and the chip already carries text-soft from chipVariants. Two faults cancelling into no colour at all.

Fixed by a shared predicate. shared/src/risk.ts states it once — riskOf for the report, dueTone for the chip, isDoneGroup for anything asking whether work has stopped — pure, with the caller passing in what today is. Eighteen places across twelve files had written it by hand.

The queries.ts half was dropped, on measurement. 359 distinct SELECTs in the server, 21 written in more than one file, and every one of the 21 a keyed lookup or an existence check. Not one is a definition. The one real definition was "at risk", and it belongs in shared, where a queries.ts could not have put it. Moving 631 statements to de-duplicate 21 one-line lookups buys tidiness and risks behaviour.

The other half was real, and is done. routes/auth.ts — 957 lines, 41 endpoints, eight concerns — is five files now: auth.ts (17 endpoints), workspaces.ts (10), and mail.ts (7), telegram.ts (4) and push.ts (3) beside the adapters they belong to.

04 — The shape being aimed at

A kernel, capabilities, adapters

Three rules, and they are the whole architecture. What they buy: a capability that can be added by adding a directory, switched off without being loaded on either side, tested against the kernel and nothing else, and removed by deleting a directory and one line. None of those is true today for any of the fifteen.

The contract is deliberately narrow. No dynamic loading and no plugin API — manifests are imported statically by one registry file, because a plugin API is a compatibility promise the project does not want yet. No new runtime dependency — the registry is an array of objects and a for loop. No change to the sync protocol or the schema — every table keeps its name and every entity keeps its registry entry; what moves is which file the entry is written in.

05 — The one edge that changes

What actually moves

Findings 2 and 3 are one mechanism seen twice: writeEntity both contains every capability's rules and imported the rules engine it calls. Both become the same thing — a hook a module registers for. The right half below is now real: onWrite exists and automation is its first user. Note what did not change: the calls stay synchronous and inside the transaction, which is the invariant withEffectsHeld exists to protect.

Step 10 was last on purpose and earned the place: 220 files moved, no behaviour changed, and by then the files being moved were already small and already owned their rules. What it bought is not the directory listing — it is that the map above is now read off the tree rather than written down beside it. Two things went wrong, which is the reason a rename this size is checked rather than trusted. lib/reactions.ts and components/reactions.tsx were unambiguous in two directories and not in one — ./reactions resolves .ts before .tsx, so every importer of the component would silently have got the helper. And env.ts worked out the repository root by counting ../.., then moved two directories deeper: the typechecker was happy, all 1 340 tests passed, and the server served no web build at all. The first was caught by comparing the resolved import graph before and after — 319 nodes, 2 004 edges, the same graph under the rename. The second by the browser walkthrough, which is far too late, so the root is found now and three runtime paths have a test.

07 — Honest limits

What this won't fix

  • repo.ts got smaller than this said it would. It is 858 lines, from 2 370. This entry used to promise a floor around 1 300 — the merge, the transaction, the tombstones, the search index and the generic effects, said to belong together. Two of them did not: the notification copy and the webhook payloads were a capability and an adapter sitting in the write path, and they left when it got a hook for them. What is left is the floor.
  • The kernel stays a single node. Modules do not change nextSeq() or the in-process bus, and step 4 deliberately does not either — inverting an import is not the same as making a call asynchronous, and only the second buys a second replica. Every trade-off architecture.md accepts is still accepted.
  • i18n stays the largest kernel module. 7 617 lines is three catalogues of 2 258 keys. Step 2 stops shipping the two nobody asked for; it does not make the catalogues smaller, and it should not — a compile error on a missing key is worth more than the file size.
  • Modules are not a permission boundary. A capability is a unit of code, not of trust. Who may see what is still canSeeProject and the workspace check in front of it, and moving visibility rules into modules must not make that check optional.
08 — What stops the map rotting

The rules, and who enforces them

scripts/modules.mjs runs in CI beside check:compose and check:figures. Seven numbered rules, plus a check that the tables in the document still match the tree — the last row below. Where a rule has an escape hatch it is named rather than counted: a threshold is a budget somebody will spend, and a named exception has to be deleted from the file by whoever fixes it. Three of the seven carry a list; all three are empty.

The checks have a suite of their own. Every rule below was proved once by breaking the tree by hand and watching the check complain — and then the proof was thrown away. scripts/checks.test.mjs is those proofs kept: a case copies the tree, breaks one thing in the copy, runs the checker and asserts what it says. Two of them break the script instead, because a walk that quietly returns nothing is the regression that would leave every check passing and every table on this page wrong.

All three allowlists are empty, and shrinking is the only direction they move: an exception is deleted by whoever fixes it, never traded for another. A new violation has no precedent to point at. Rule 1 stopped being a list at all in step 10 — the path is the claim, so belonging to two modules is impossible and belonging to none fails the build. Rule 3 was lib/ below routes/, which was the same idea in the only two directories that then existed; stated about a directory it started covering the client and found four violations there and in the MCP adapter within minutes. All four are fixed.

The direction rules, and which of them are rules now

The rules about direction became checkable once the modules were in directories. The first measurement said 108 crossings, which was two counting mistakes on top of a real problem. A routes/ file composes — that is what a route is, wherever it sits — so counting a route's imports as ring dependencies made the kernel look like it depended on nine capabilities when what it had was nine endpoints. And an import type is erased when the file compiles: it couples the build, not the run. 108 becomes 35 that survive compilation and 5 that do not.

Nothing points outward any more. KNOWN_OUTWARD is empty, and rule 5 keeps it that way. The last row moves the other way on purpose: an adapter is supposed to lean on a capability, and it does so 8 times now precisely because the outward edges were turned round. The way out was the same every time — the inner ring says what it needs, the outer one registers. There are 8 ports across 7 modules now, and all 8 are filled. Four of the fixes were not inversions at all but things filed in the wrong place, and those four are the interesting ones.

Rule 7 turned that table into a check. That second half is the one no other rule catches — a kernel module filling another kernel module's port passes rules 5 and 6 and is still not a port. And a @port tag on anything but an exported function would drop a row here and three figures with it, silently, so the tags are counted twice and a disagreement is named.

A format filed as a provider

3 of the 5 exceptionskernel/mail, 2 files4 callers, 1 sends mail

Both true — and both needed the shape, not the client. kernel/mail holds the shape; adapters/mail keeps the sockets, the RFC 5321 conversation and the retries.

Four things ask what an address is and one of them is sending mail: an account is named by an address whether or not this instance can post to one.

2

A dialog filed as an edge

2 of the 5 exceptionsmodules/share, 155 lines3 screens embed it

The old note had the diagnosis and stopped there: share is an adapter on the server, where it renders a board for whoever holds the link, and product UI on the client, where it makes the link. The client half imports the design system, the store and the session and nothing outside the process, which is a screen — so it is a capability. 3

The exception was hiding a cycle

rule 6 now covers every ring0 module cycles

Emptying the list is what showed why the rule was too narrow. The three named kernel-to-adapter edges were not only arrows pointing the wrong way: with them, kernel/platform, kernel/i18n and adapter/mail formed a module cycle — excused by name under rule 5, and invisible to rule 6, which was only looking at capabilities. Rule 6 covers every ring now, and after rule 5 a cycle cannot cross a ring boundary, so any that appears is inside one ring and this is what finds it. Re-adding a single edge reports both faults at once.

Two more were misfilings of the same kind, fixed earlier: AppShell.tsx composes work, chat and the guide, which is a shell and not a design system, and zip.ts imports node:zlib and nothing else, which is a format and not an adapter.

Depending on each other is the second question and the answer is not zero. work imports planning because the timeline view of a task list is a Gantt chart; no inversion changes that, and a registry invented per one-off relationship would be the counted budget this document argues against, in a costume. What they can be is ordered, and they already are — 17 imports across 15 module pairs, generated from the import graph rather than remembered:

Is there a fifth ring? No.

The table raises it: capabilities are plainly not peers, so perhaps the ones others lean on are a ring of their own, between the kernel and the rest. Three things say no, and the last is decisive.

1

There is no stratum

16 capabilities15 edgesmost leaned-on: 35 leaned on by nobody

A ring is not "the important ones", it is a set that depends on nothing above it and that the things above it depend on. The most leaned-on capability is pages, and 3 of the other 15 lean on it. Five — automation, chat, infrastructure, intake, kpis — are leaned on by nobody at all, and two of those lean on nothing either.

There are 3 606 ways to split this graph into a lower part that depends on nothing above it and an upper part. Every one of them was tried. The lower part that the most capabilities lean on is budgets, pages and share: 3 modules that 5 of the rest lean on. That is the strongest case available for a fifth ring, and it is a sparse graph with a few edges rather than a foundation with a product on top.

2

The candidate disqualifies itself

work → ai-reviewwork → pageswork → planningwork → sharework → time

work looks like the foundation — it is the biggest domain and other capabilities lean on it — and it leans on five of them. Put work in a ring below capabilities and all five of those imports point outward, which is what rule 5 forbids: a ring that breaks the ring rule on the day it is drawn is not a ring. Move the five down with it and the split is legal again — but it is then one of the 3 606 counted above, and not the best of them.

3

A ring has to buy a rule

Each of the four exists because it makes something checkable that otherwise is not: what is always present, what a switch can remove, what faces outside, what merely composes. A fifth would have to make "capabilities are ordered" checkable — and that is already rule 6, as acyclicity, which needs no ring to say it.

What changes instead is the description. This analysis called capabilities peers. They are not: they are a sparse partial order, 16 members and 15 edges, no cycles. Smaller and truer than a fifth ring, and it costs nothing to keep honest.

A correction. An earlier version of this page put work at "level 2 with seven capabilities under it" and read that as work sitting closer to the kernel. The number was a longest-dependency-chain depth, which measures what a module stands on, not what stands on it — precisely backwards for the question being asked. Counted the right way round, the most depended-upon capability is pages, which leans on nothing. The table above is generated from the graph now, so the next person to ask gets the measurement rather than the memory.
